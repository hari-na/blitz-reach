# Pre-call prep: sector-specific probes

**How to use these: do not ask them cold.** They are probes to deploy once the topic
comes up naturally in Block 3, so that when they say "there was a weight issue" you can
go three levels deeper instead of nodding. Asking them cold leads the witness and you
will get your own hypothesis read back to you.

The value is vocabulary. If you use their words, they stop explaining basics and start
telling you what actually went wrong.

---

## Limestone (quarrying / crushing / trading)

### Why this is an excellent subject

Limestone is a textbook case of the structure we care about: a bulk commodity sold by
weight and by grade, where **the buyer independently measures both on arrival.** That
means two parties hold different records of the same delivery, by design, on every single
truck. Reconciliation is not an edge case here, it is the business.

Typical buyers are cement plants, steel plants (as flux), glass, construction,
agriculture and paper. Large, sophisticated, portal-driven counterparties against a
seller running on Tally and WhatsApp. That asymmetry is where the money leaks.

### The fracture points, in priority order

**1. Weighbridge mismatch.** The truck is weighed out at the quarry and weighed again at
the buyer's gate. The numbers differ. Probe:

> When a truck gets weighed at their end, does the weight ever come back different from
> your slip? What happens then? Who eats the difference? When was the last time?

**2. Grade and moisture claims.** The buyer tests on arrival (CaO content, moisture,
silica) and reduces the rate or rejects the load. This is the biggest one. Probe:

> Do buyers test the material when it arrives? Has a buyer ever paid you at a lower rate
> than agreed because of the test result? How do you find out? Can you challenge it?

**3. Unilateral debit notes.** This is the probe I would spend the most time on. Large
buyers deduct for short weight, grade, moisture or delay and simply pay less. The seller
discovers it when the payment lands. Probe:

> When payment comes in, is it ever less than you invoiced? How do you find out why?
> Do they send you something explaining the deduction, or do you have to go digging?
> Have you ever just accepted a deduction because chasing it was not worth the effort?

That last question is the one. The answer is a number nobody has ever counted.

**4. Transporter billing.** Freight billed per tonne or per trip, against trips the
seller dispatched. Probe:

> How do you check a transporter's bill is right? Have you ever paid for a trip that did
> not happen, or the wrong tonnage? How would you know?

**5. Royalty, permits and transit passes.** Royalty is payable per tonne to the state,
with transit passes or e-permits per load and district mining office paperwork. There is
also a live, genuinely contested GST question about royalty under reverse charge
following the Supreme Court's MADA ruling. **Do not assert any of this.** Ask:

> What do you have to file or submit for the mining side? Royalty, permits, transit
> passes? Who handles it, and what happens if a load moves without the paperwork?
> Has your CA ever raised the GST treatment on royalty with you?

If he lights up on the royalty GST question, that is a real and expensive live issue, and
an owner who has been through it will talk for ten minutes.

**6. Transit loss.** Twenty tonnes leaves, nineteen point six arrives. Probe:

> Does the quantity that arrives ever just come up short? Is there an allowance for that,
> and who decided it?

**7. E-way bills.** One per consignment above the threshold. High frequency, rule-bound,
and a load moving without one is a real penalty. Probe:

> Who raises the e-way bills? How many a day? Has one ever been wrong or missing when a
> truck was already on the road?

### The one answer that would make this a product

If he says something like "payment comes short and I don't always know why, and chasing
it costs more than the deduction," stop and dig for ten minutes. That is a measurable
monthly leak, caused entirely by two parties holding different records, in a business
too small to afford anyone to fix it. It is the same problem Zamp sells to the Fortune
500 and it is sitting in your friend's bank account every month.

## The general pattern, for any business

If you end up talking to someone whose sector I have not prepped, the structure to hunt
for is always the same three questions:

1. **Who else measures the same thing you measure?** Weight, hours, units, quality,
   arrival time. Wherever both sides measure independently, there is a dispute.
2. **Who can reduce your payment without asking you first?** That is where the
   unmeasured leak lives.
3. **What document carries the truth, and who reads it?** If the answer is a PDF, a
   portal export or a settlement file that a human skims once a month, there is a product
   in it.

## Travel (agency / tour operator / DMC)

### Why this is the best subject of the three

Travel is almost pure reconciliation. The agent sits between a customer and a stack of
suppliers (airlines, hotels, consolidators, DMCs, a payment gateway) and **every one of
those counterparties can adjust what they pay or charge after the fact.** Money moves
before the service is delivered, gets clawed back afterwards, and arrives in settlement
batches that bear no resemblance to individual bookings.

It also has the single highest chargeback rate of any industry, and chargeback filing is
a workflow Zamp already sells to an enterprise customer. If you find the SMB version of
that pain here, the line to Zamp's product is one sentence long.

### First, work out which kind he is

The probes differ. Ask in Block 1:

> Do you sell mostly flights, hotels, or packages? And is it walk-in customers, corporates,
> or other agents?

- **IATA ticketing agent** (sells flights, settles through BSP): go straight to ADMs below.
  This is the richest case.
- **Tour operator / DMC** (builds and runs packages): focus on supplier payables, room
  nights and the GST and TCS questions.
- **B2C agency reselling OTA or consolidator inventory:** focus on commission and refunds.
- **Corporate travel:** focus on credit, receivables and invoice disputes.

### The fracture points, in priority order

**1. Agency Debit Memos.** If he is an IATA agent, this is the whole conversation. Airlines
unilaterally issue ADMs to claw money back for fare rule breaches, wrong booking class,
name changes, commission errors, sometimes months after travel. The agent finds out when
the memo appears in the settlement file. Probe:

> Do you get ADMs? When was the last one, and what was it for? How do you find out one
> has been raised? Can you dispute it, and have you ever won? How many do you just pay
> because fighting it is not worth the time?

That last question is the same question as the limestone debit notes, and it is the one
that produces a number nobody has counted.

**2. Short commission.** Promised commission or markup versus what actually lands.
Hotels pay commission 30 to 90 days after checkout, often short, sometimes never. Probe:

> How do you know whether you have been paid the commission you earned on a booking? Who
> checks? Do you chase the ones that never arrive, or is there a point where you stop?
> Could you tell me today how much commission is outstanding?

If he cannot answer that last part without opening three files, that is the answer.

**3. Refunds and cancellations.** Customer cancels, the agent owes the customer a refund,
and has to claim it back from the airline or hotel, which takes weeks and arrives short.
Probe:

> Walk me through the last cancellation. Who paid the customer back, and when? How long
> did the supplier take to refund you? Did the amount match? How do you keep track of
> refunds you are still owed?

**4. Settlement versus booking.** The gateway settles in batches minus fees; BSP settles
fortnightly; nothing maps one to one onto a booking. Probe:

> When money lands from the card gateway or from BSP, how do you work out which bookings
> it covers? Does anyone tie that back, or do you trust the total?

**5. Chargebacks.** Customer disputes a card payment, sometimes for a trip they actually
took. Probe:

> Has a customer ever disputed a payment with their bank? What happened? Who put together
> the evidence, and how long did it take? Did you win?

**6. Supplier payables and room nights.** What the hotel invoices versus what was actually
used, with no-shows, early checkouts and upgrades in between. Probe:

> When a hotel bills you, do you check it against what the guest actually used? Has a
> hotel ever billed for a no-show or a night that was not taken?

**7. GST and TCS.** Travel has genuinely messy treatment: a tour operator rate without
input credit, a different rate on air ticket commission, place of supply rules, and TCS
on overseas tour packages that has moved around in recent years. **Do not assert any of
the rates.** Ask:

> How does GST work for your kind of booking? Has the TCS on overseas packages caused you
> problems? Who handles that, you or your CA, and has it ever gone wrong?

**8. Credit and float.** Corporates pay in 45 days while suppliers want money upfront.
Probe:

> Do you pay suppliers before your customers pay you? How do you decide who to pay when
> money is tight? Who chases the corporates, and how?

### The one answer that would make this a product

Anything of the shape "money comes back less than it should and I can't always tell why,
and past a certain amount I stop chasing it." Whether that comes from an ADM, a short
commission, a partial refund or a chargeback does not matter. It is the same structural
problem as the limestone debit notes: **a counterparty adjusts the number unilaterally,
and the business has no system that notices.**

If you hear it in limestone and again in travel, two unrelated industries, you do not
have an anecdote any more. You have the wedge.
