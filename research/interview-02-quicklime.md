# Interview 02: Arnav Malkani, quicklime processing and manufacturing

Date: 2026-11-15. Recorded, transcript available. Call ended partway through the guide.

## Who

Not a limestone miner. **A processor.** He buys quicklime lumps from mines in Jaipur and
Jodhpur, grinds them, treats them with chemicals and coating materials, packages and
sells.

- **Three products:** quicklime powder (the volume product), Master Batch (made from his
  own quicklime powder), and calcium filler (a loading and flow additive for plastics).
- **82% of his market is recycled plastic**: carry bags, garbage bags, small furniture
  mouldings, toys, cups.
- **Two plants.** Udaipur dispatches ~1,200 tons/month. Bhiwadi ~900 tons/month.
  Roughly 3 to 4 trucks a day out of Udaipur, 2 to 3 out of Bhiwadi.
- **Concentrated clientele.** Largest client takes 32% of capacity, smallest 4%.
- **Tally.** One accountant handles billing, invoicing and GST. A CA handles the heavier
  tax work.
- Udaipur has **his own weighbridge**; Bhiwadi uses public ones near the industrial area.
- Travels with each truck: invoice, e-way bill, gate pass (the gate pass is his own
  record, not required).
- **45-day credit** is the industry norm. Some clients pay vehicle-to-vehicle, some
  monthly as a lump.
- **Price-setter, not price-taker.** Pricing follows limestone and petcoke, and he sells
  above market range on quality. Buyers do not compute his number.

## Where the hypothesis landed

**The unilateral deduction pattern is largely absent, and for a clear reason: he sets the
price and he holds the quality advantage.** Unlike the travel subject, nobody computes
his entitlement for him.

What does exist:

- **Quality complaints: 2 to 3 times a year.** When one lands he issues a debit note to
  compensate. So the debit note mechanism exists but the frequency is too low to build on.
- **Buyers share their Tally statement**, so there is a counterparty ledger exchange.
  **He agreed to send one** (he will pick a small client).
- **Payment-to-invoice matching is already solved.** He uses a tool called **Reportec**
  that converts bank statements into Tally XML; his accountant runs it, a month of
  statements takes 3 to 4 hours, and he says spotting a missed payment is easy. Worth
  recording as a negative: this part of the problem is already commoditized in Indian SMB
  and is not a place to build.

## The one real exposure, and it is measurable

**Weighbridge variance, absorbed monthly, never counted.**

- His slip and the buyer's slip differ **"many times."** Causes he named: weighbridge
  faults, vehicle issues, and **diesel weight** (the truck arrives with a full tank and
  leaves empty, so the tare shifts).
- Industry tolerance is 100 to 200 kg. "If it's less, it's okay. If it's more, it's also
  okay."
- **When it goes against him, he pays.** There is also a bag count: bags are 25 or 50 kg,
  and if bags come up short he absorbs it.
- Frequency: **"every month."**
- His own assessment: **"I don't pay that much attention to it. It's a really small detail
  in my business. It's nothing to work upon."**

**The honest reading, and the open question.** He said the variance runs both ways, so it
may well net out near zero, in which case there is nothing here. But the **bag shortage
case is one-directional** by his own description, and nobody has ever checked whether the
weight variance is symmetric or biased.

Scale: ~2,100 tons a month across roughly 150 to 180 loads. If each load carried even
100 kg of net unrecovered variance, that is ~15 to 18 tons a month. **Do not estimate the
rupee value.** He has both numbers already (his weighbridge slips and the buyer slips he
receives), so the question is whether anyone has ever added up a single month.

The only reason he is confident it does not matter is that he has never measured it. Same
structure as the travel subject's Maldives settlement, except here **the data already
exists on both sides.**

## Things he volunteered that we did not ask about

These are more interesting than most of the answers, and per the method, what someone
describes unprompted and in detail is what is on their mind.

### 1. He is opening his own mines next month

"I've opened my own mines... next month's production." Vertical integration, starting
imminently. That brings an entirely new compliance surface he has never dealt with:
per-tonne royalty, transit passes, the mining department. Completely unexplored, and
it is a live transition rather than a hypothetical.

### 2. The four-hop order status chain

He described this in more detail than anything else in the call, unprompted:

> Client calls an account manager → account manager tells the factory supervisor →
> supervisor tells the head of labour → head of labour starts the order.

Normal lead time is **3 to 4 days.** And when orders back up, the same chain runs in
reverse: labour tells supervisor, supervisor tells account manager, account manager tells
the client it will slip by two or three days.

Two account managers handle the small clients. **He personally handles the big ones.**

That is a human telephone chain, four hops deep, in both directions, carrying order
status for a business shipping six trucks a day. Nobody asked a single question about it.

### 3. Sample follow-up with prospective clients

On new-client samples: "some reply within the day... some people take 7 or 8 days, some
people take a month, **some people ghost you.**"

That was the only moment of real feeling in the call, and it is a pipeline leak he named
himself.

### 4. Client concentration

One client is 32% of capacity. Not an automation problem, but it is the business risk he
is actually carrying.

## What the call did not cover

The call ended partway through the guide. Entirely unasked:

- **The calendar walk.** "Walk me through last Monday" was never asked. This is the
  highest-yield question in the whole method and we skipped it for this subject.
- **The whole money and priority block.** What he would hand over, what it costs today,
  whether he has bought software before, what caps growth.
- **The delegation and trust block.** What happens when the accountant is away, what he
  checks personally, what would let him stop.
- **Royalty, transit passes, mining department compliance** (now urgent, given the mines).
- Transporter billing got tangled. Resolved: trucks he sends to his *suppliers* are his
  own; trucks going out to *clients* are not his.

## Comparison with interview 01

| | Travel DMC | Quicklime |
|---|---|---|
| Who sets the number | The resort | **He does** |
| Is it reconciled | Never, in 9 years | Bank matching automated via Reportec |
| Loss visibility | Silent, denied | **Admitted and absorbed** |
| Owner's account of it | "Everything is perfect" | "It's nothing to work upon" |
| Frequency of the loss | Annual, unmeasured | **Monthly, unmeasured** |

Both dismiss it. The travel subject denies it exists; Arnav concedes it exists and judges
it immaterial. **His version is better, because he is probably right that each instance is
trivial, and nobody has ever checked whether the sum is.**

The deduction thesis is weakening across two interviews. What is strengthening is
something more useful: **in both businesses the owner has an accurate view of each
individual event and no view at all of the aggregate.** That is a measurement gap, not a
processing gap, and it is exactly the gap an outside diagnostic closes.
