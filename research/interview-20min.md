# The 20-minute version

Use this live. The long guide is now a question bank to raid, not a script.

A short interview is not a truncated long one. You ask fewer questions and you dig much
harder on each. **The probes matter more than the questions.** Nine questions, five
blocks, and a set of follow-ups you can apply to any answer.

---

## Before you dial

**Say this, and get a yes on tape:**

> Do you mind if I record this? Only so I don't have to take notes while we talk.

Then, on the recording: today's date, their name, their business. Five seconds, saves
you an hour later.

**Frame it right:**

> I'm researching how businesses your size actually handle back office paperwork.
> I'm not selling anything. I want to understand how it really works.

**The one rule:** you should talk for less than a fifth of this call. Every time you want
to explain something, ask a question instead. Silence is fine. Let them fill it.

---

## The universal probes

These are the whole method. Apply them to any answer, repeatedly, until you hit a
specific event with a number attached.

- **When was the last time that happened?**
- **Walk me through that one.**
- **How did you find out?**
- **What happened next?**
- **How long did that take?**
- **Who else got involved?**
- **What did that cost you?**
- **Can you show me?**
- **Why?** (three times, if you can get away with it)

If an answer contains the words "usually," "generally," or "we try to," you have been
handed a policy, not an event. Probe until you get the last actual instance.

---

## Block 1 — Frame (0:00 to 2:00)

**1. In two lines, what does the business do and who pays you?**

**2. Roughly how many invoices go out a month, and how many come in?**

That is all the context you need. Do not spend more time here. You can ask about software
and headcount afterwards over text.

---

## Block 2 — The real week (2:00 to 7:00)

**3. Walk me through last Monday. What did you actually do?**

> Probe: And the rest of that week? What did you do on Monday that you'd also done the
> Monday before, almost exactly the same way?

**4. When was the last time you worked late or gave up a weekend to this stuff? What
were you doing?**

> Probe: How often does that happen? What makes it happen?

**Why this block:** question 3's follow-up is the single highest-yield question in the
whole project. Repetition on a fixed cadence is the definition of automatable work, and
asking it about a specific remembered day surfaces repetition they have stopped noticing.
Question 4 finds pain severe enough to cost them personal time, which beats anything they
say about their priorities.

---

## Block 3 — Follow one rupee (7:00 to 14:00)

This is the longest block. Pick the direction that fits their business (money coming in
for a services or B2B business, money going out for a distributor or manufacturer) and
follow it end to end. Do not do both.

**5. Take the last invoice you sent. Who made it, from what, and how did it reach them?
Then how do you find out whether it got paid?**

> Probe hard on the negative case: And how do you find out when it has *not* been paid?
> Does something tell you, or do you notice?
>
> Then: When money lands in the bank, how do you work out which invoices it covers?

**6. When was the last time your numbers and somebody else's numbers did not agree?**

> Probe: How did you spot it? How long to sort out? Did anybody pay for the difference?
>
> If they sell through an aggregator or marketplace: When a payout comes in, how do you
> check it is right? Has it ever been wrong?

**Why this block:** the happy path in any surviving business is already smooth, or the
business would be dead. All the cost is in the cases that do not match, and the owner
cannot see those as a pattern because each one feels like a one-off. Question 6 is the
one that finds the pattern. If you only get one good answer in the whole call, make it
this one.

---

## Block 4 — Delegation and trust (14:00 to 18:00)

**7. Have you ever tried to hire someone to take this off you?**

> If yes: How did it go? What went wrong?
> If no: What do you still do yourself that you know you should have handed off by now?
> Then, either way: Why has that not happened?

**8. What do you check twice because you do not quite trust it?**

> Probe: Has it ever actually been wrong? When?

**Why this block:** question 7 is the commercial core of the interview. Never tried to
delegate it means it does not hurt enough to pay for. Tried and failed means the reason
it failed is your product specification. Question 8 finds the gap between what they
verify and what actually breaks, which is pure waste, and it is the safest possible place
for a first deployment because automating a check that has never failed has almost no
downside.

---

## Block 5 — Priority and budget (18:00 to 20:00)

**9. If I could take one of the things we talked about off your plate completely, which
one would you pick, and what does getting it done cost you today?**

> Probe: Why that one and not the others? Have you ever paid for software to fix it?
> What happened to it?

**Why last:** this question changes the tone of the conversation, so it goes at the end
where it cannot contaminate anything. It gives you the priority ordering from the person
who would actually pay, and the cost answer is the only honest pricing anchor you will get.

---

## The close (and the artifacts)

> What did I not ask about that I should have?

Then, before you hang up:

> Can you WhatsApp me the last invoice you sent and [the payout file / the sheet you
> track payments in]? Nothing confidential, just want to see the real format.

Asking for artifacts *after* the conversation works far better than during. By then they
know what you care about and it feels like helping rather than disclosing.

And:

> Can I come back to you once I've looked at this properly?

The second conversation is where you find out if you were right. It matters more than
this one.

---

## Timing discipline

Glance at the clock at 7 and at 14 minutes. If you are behind, cut from Block 3, not from
Block 4. The delegation question is worth more than a complete money trail.

If they go off on a tangent, **follow it.** Tangents are usually where the real pain is,
because the thing they volunteer unprompted is the thing on their mind. Only cut it if
it has run two minutes with no specific event in it.

If the call is going well at 20 minutes, do not stop. Say "I know I said twenty minutes,
happy to let you go whenever" and let them decide. Most people keep going.

---

## Do more of these, not longer ones

Four twenty-minute calls beat two forty-minute calls. You are looking for a pattern
across businesses, and a pattern needs at least three instances. Depth on one person
tells you about one person.

Target three to five conversations, and bias toward people whose businesses differ. If
the same exception shows up in a restaurant, a distributor and a clinic, that is a
product. If it shows up once, that is an anecdote.

## Recording notes

- **Audio only.** Less intimidating than video and nobody tidies their voice the way
  they tidy their face.
- **Record the whole call, including the opening,** so the consent is captured in the file.
- A plain phone call with a recorder app is fine. If it is a WhatsApp or regular voice
  call and recording is awkward, switch to Google Meet or Zoom, both of which record
  natively and will transcribe as a bonus.
- Hand me the audio file in whatever format it lands in, plus one line on who they are
  and why you picked them. I will transcribe it, pull out every task with its frequency
  and cost, and fill in the log template.
- Name the files `interview-01-<firstname>.<ext>` so the ordering survives.
